Geoffrey Lewis

8 August 2026 Discovery By Josh

It's great when I hear a song, enjoy it for its atmosphere, and then read how the artist came to it from a different angle.

"Paranoid" by Geoffrey Lewis is just that. It grabbed me this week. Although I can certainly hear the DNA of Sunny Day Real Estate or American Football in the song, the feel of the track is what drew me in.

"Paranoid" tackles heavy subject matter, it is inspired by the anxiety of growing up in a world where public violence has become normalised.

That rawness, directness and authenticity of the song are reflected in how the track was made. Entirely written, performed, recorded, and produced in his Nashville home studio, the song is built around an analog set up, a Korg drum machine, tube amplifiers, and live instruments. I think for me, this is where the feel comes from. Because of this direct artist led production style, the track reminds me of the proto-electronic acts like Suicide or Silver Apples. Similarly, although sonically different, it has the same kind of clash between guitars and electronics of Liars.

Geoffrey's college friends provide the bass and backing vocals, and I really love how the song builds from the initial guitar riff, layered with those understated but highly effective vocal backings. The custom Korg beat drives the song, while the guitar motif responds with a rhythmic edge.

Lewis sings with a measured, understated, quality that makes the paranoia feel even more intense. He keeps the emotion just below the surface, and that restraint is exactly what makes it engaging.

This song sounds exactly like its title. The song does, indeed, sound paranoid, in a good way!

Liars

8 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Liars

They Threw Us All in a Trench and Stuck a Monument on Top has still got to be one of the greatest album titles of all time.

It was by Liars, I am not sure about their propensity for the truth but they were an amazing band.

Formed in Brooklyn at the turn of the millennium but fronted by Australian lead singer Angus Andrew, they were a vital band of that era. They were part of a set of bands to bring post punk back but mix it with electronic production to create a dance punk sound. Which was a much needed antidote to the polished sounds of the late 90's.

My first pick for the archive is the equally brilliantly named "Mr You're On Fire Mr".

Mr You're On Fire Mr by Liars

Nine years later, and my second pick is "Scissor", a completely different beast of a track and the opener to their 2010 album, Sisterworld.

Scissor by Liars

A year after that release, I saw them play an unbelievable set at the "I'll Be Your Mirror" festival at London's Alexandra Palace. Curated by Portishead and put together by All Tomorrow's Parties, it was a legendary gig. ATP might have eventually gone bust amid accusations of profiteering, but the line-ups they pulled together were outstanding. I'll likely keep referring back to that festival on this blog, but Liars were absolutely a highlight of the weekend!

The Cuts

6 August 2026 Discovery By Josh

I have been very lucky to attend some memorable gigs over the years. One surreal, brilliant experience was watching The Strokes while stood right in front of a massive dinosaur skeleton at the Natural History Museum in London.

Now, I don't have The Strokes in the 1000 Songs archive, but they were undeniably pivotal. They heralded a massive explosion of brilliant guitar music throughout the early to mid 2000s, freeing bands just to be bands and make catchy, guitar driven tunes without overthinking it.

So, in that tradition of simply making joyful, infectious guitar music, I was delighted to receive the latest single from The Cuts, titled "Fire with Fire".

The Cuts hail from Paisley, Scotland. The core trio of Neil Walker, Kieran Docherty, and James Robertson first joined forces years ago before drifting down separate paths, only to reunite in 2025 and enlist drummer Craig Brown to complete the lineup. Refreshingly, the band embraces a playful, tongue-in-cheek irreverence while keeping their songwriting genuinely sharp.

"Fire with Fire" was written by Neil Walker late last year and recorded on analogue equipment at Glasgow’s intimate Green Door Studio. That was a fantastic call. It captures all the raw excitement and energy of seeing a band live in a small room, so you aren't left trying to pick out a brilliant song through dense layers of over production.

Musically, the song rolls along with effortless momentum. For me, it kicks off with a sharp, punchy Buzzcocks type vibe to begin with, but quickly builds with the energy and attitude of Bloc Party before launching into a brilliant, peak Franz Ferdinand strength of a chorus.

That chorus is so relentlessly catchy it will lodge itself in your brain until you can't help singing it aloud. In fact, I wager, you’ll find yourself standing in a queue, singing, "I've got to fight fire with fire." A stranger will look at you confused and say, "Excuse me?" To which you'll have to reply deadpan, "Oh, sorry, it's just a song stuck in my head. You should check it out, it's by a band called The Cuts."

Fire with Fire by The Cuts is out on 28th August 2026.

Bessie Smith

5 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Bessie Smith

Bessie Smith was Blues. Bessie Smith was folk. Bessie Smith was Jazz. Bessie Smith was Punk.

She also released a lot of records prior to 1930. Which is not particularly useful when your archive starts in 1930.

She was born in Chattanooga, Tennessee, she survived her childhood by busking on street corners.

She eventually rose to become the highest-paid Black entertainer of her day. She even had her own custom rail carriage.

She championed the African-American working class. She was a true pioneer who helped invent the market for "race records" and female blues singers in the 1920s.

She incorporated biting commentary on poverty, the convict lease system, capital punishment, and female empowerment into her songs.

To represent her in the archive, I’ve chosen "Blue Blue." from 1931.

Blue Blue by Bessie Smith

But I'd also recommend reading about her amazing life and listening to some of those brilliant 1920s records. Just proving to me genre never really existed!

Gomez

3 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Gomez

Hans Zimmer (he of the Going for Gold theme fame) is taking over the BBC Maida Vale studios. It's the end of an era. One of my favourite albums, Hatful of Hollow by The Smiths, features tracks recorded there, and it's the site of Delia Derbyshire's electronic triumphs. It's also famously where Bing Crosby made his last ever recording in 1977, just days before he died. Fontaines DCs final session there was broadcast today on 6 Music.

However, for me, it was where I saw Gomez (with Chilly Gonzales playing, and Colin Murray booking us in on the door). Gomez were such an important band for me. In this 1000 Songs project, I have engaged in a bit of late-1990s revisionism. For me, as someone who likes British guitar music, the era at the time wasn't particularly good for it, especially just after 1997.

Gomez were important because they decided to look way back and do something different with it when others tried to let Britpop limp on. Hailing from Southport, Merseyside, they were a unique outfit comprising Ian Ball, Paul Blackburn, Tom Gray, Ben Ottewell, and Olly Peacock. They brilliantly juggled three different singers and four songwriters. Their sound was a complete anomaly at the time, effortlessly blending blues, folk, and psychedelic experimental rock.

Bring It On felt very different. Looking back on it nearly 30 years later, for me personally, it hasn't dated too well. But it was authentic, raw, and much-needed, especially compared to what was on TV. It was actually won the Mercury Music Prize in 1998.

For the archive, I had a choice to make. I was considering "Whippin' Piccadilly" or even the later "Shot Shot".

Shot Shot by Gomez

But for me, it was the opening track to Bring It On,"Get Miles", which told me they were trying to do something that others just weren't doing at the time.

Get Miles by Gomez

The Fireballs

1 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: The Fireballs

The Fireballs embody a bit of raw 1950s for me. Formed in Raton, New Mexico in 1957, they started as an instrumental group driven by George Tomsco's lead guitar.

They had some great early instrumental releases, most notably 1959's "Torquay".

Torquay by The Fireballs

I also find it highly amusing that that Torquay is also a town in Devon.

A view...you say madam how about ...a bunch of New Mexico teenagers playing some twang!

Yes, absolutely nothing screams rock and roll rebellion quite like the English Riviera! (Although it didn't stop Metronomy literally calling an album that.)

Anyway...Norman Petty, who ran the Clovis studio where The Fireballs recorded, was also Buddy Holly’s producer. After Holly died, Petty obtained the rights to Holly's early acoustic demos and had The Fireballs overdub them throughout the 1960s. 1960s DIY remix culture at its finest!

Now, you might notice Buddy Holly himself isn't in the 1000 Songs archive. The Fireballs are. For me they kind of represent the pioneers who actually built a sound, and that is why they fully deserve this spot.

Massey's Cross

31 July 2026 Discovery By Josh

As part of the 1000 Songs project, I'm doing one review a week of new releases from emerging artists. Following up on my recent piece about the band untitled, this week I am spotlighting Massey's Cross.

Hailing from Calgary, Alberta, this duo brews a potent blend of classic rock and blues, laced with jazz and folk sensibilities. Their upcoming EP, "Unbroken After the Storm" (dropping this September), delivers absolutely everything you crave from a classic rock record: heavy grooves, brilliant guitar work, and deeply personal storytelling.

1000 Songs is all about tracks that immediately grab you by the collar, and their opener, "Donner Party and Sadly Forgotten," does exactly that. What I love most is how it captures a massive, Zeppelin-esque swagger while maintaining the gritty, authentic production I obsessed over during my early-2000s blues-rock phase. The energy channels the early days of The Black Keys, that raw, unpolished magic where a duo locks in and conjures an absolute wall of sound.

While the opener might be my personal favorite, you need to stick around for the whole ride. The EP naturally swells into the kind of epic, sprawling soundscapes found on the very best classic rock records, so look no further than the track "Never Odd or Even" for proof.

Whenever I listen to a new release, I want to understand the engine driving it. The band explained to me that, at its core, the EP explores the psychological terrain that follows a life-altering event. It’s not about the event itself, it’s about the aftermath, the trauma, the upheaval, the loss, the reinvention, and the brutal confrontation with the truth, events that, unfortunately, seem to happen more frequently as we get older. The main idea behind the record is that you emerge from the storm neither triumphant nor defeated, but simply changed.

The EP traces this slow, cinematic process of coming to terms with that change. Massey's Cross describes the project not just as a collection of songs, but as a single, dramatic gesture. After listening to it, I wholeheartedly agree.

A great addition to the 1000 Songs radar.

Lord Kitchener

31 July 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Lord Kitchener

If we are talking about songs that capture a specific moment in time, which is basically the entire point of this 1000 Songs project, it is genuinely impossible to ignore Aldwyn Roberts, better known to the world as Lord Kitchener, or simply "Kitch."

He wasn't just a musician; he was the undisputed Grand Master of Calypso.

There are three key things you really need to understand about him to get why he's so vital to this archive.

Firstly, the man literally soundtracked history. In 1948, he bought a ticket on the HMT Empire Windrush. When the ship pulled into Tilbury Docks, bringing some of the first post-war West Indian immigrants to the UK, a reporter for Pathé News shoved a microphone in his face. Right there, on the spot, Kitchener sang a specially written song into the camera. No studio, no auto-tune, just a man and a heavy dose of optimism stepping into the freezing English breeze. That song was "London Is the Place for Me."

Secondly, he became the absolute voice of those first 5,000 West Indian migrants. Imagine leaving the Caribbean for a grey, post-war Britain. His music was a vital lifeline. It spoke of a home they desperately missed, but also documented the reality of the life they were trying to build.

Thirdly, he was a relentless innovator. He didn't just have a brief stint in the UK and fade away. He eventually moved back to Trinidad and completely dominated the calypso scene. We're talking winning the Road March competition 10 times. He ran his own calypso tent, nurtured a whole new generation of artists, and basically helped pioneer the transition into Soca music in the 70s.

And of course I put in...

London is the Place for Me by Lord Kitchener

Moondog

29 July 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Moondog

Moondog was one of the artists I only found out about after starting 1000 Songs.

Firstly, you can't go wrong with referencing the moon. Surely. It's like the cooler sun. While we are all projecting our happy vibes onto the sun, I swear it is instead looking at itself in the moon, thinking it's fronting Echo and the Bunnymen.

Anyway...

Moondog (born Louis Thomas Hardin) was a blind composer and musician who famously "lived" on the streets of New York dressed as a Viking! I think this is a bit of lore. But he wasn't just an eccentric street performer. He made brilliant, complex music, and not only that, he was the inventor of his own instruments, most notably a triangular percussion instrument called the "trimba."

Truly innovative, he was the inspiration for legendary minimalist artists such as Philip Glass and Steve Reich, who actually championed his work and viewed him as a peer.

I absolutely love the looping, repetitive phrases in his work, and the production quality is just unbelievable for the 1950s. It sounds so far ahead of its time.

For the archive, I have gone with "Theme", "Dog Trot", and "Oboe Round".

Oboe Round by Moondog

I loved Ormside Street. It had this brilliant industrial estate aesthetic. Used to be Friends by Big Joanie

Why You Won't Find Elbow Here - Except..Well...Here

25 July 2026 Methodology By Josh

When I hear a piece of recorded music, I always think of the effort it must have taken to reach my ears. To think that someone, or a group of people, had to learn how to do it, record it, not be self-conscious about sharing it with other people, and those people then investing a degree of marketing and plugging. I think my main point is that there has to be a degree of skill involved in making it. The rest of the stuff probably has a set of mechanisms that can be enacted. So, taking the skill of making something away in the first place removes the quality control, which means my neighbour's music makes my ears bleed.

Elbow aren't that. So, Elbow. Elbow. Elbow. There is skill in what they do. Every week I used to go to Koko in Camden (formerly Camden Palace) to watch bands being filmed for a TV show (I can't remember what it was called), and to my great pleasure, there was Guy Garvey and the lads. To my greater pleasure, this was the first time I heard "One Day Like This" and "Grounds for Divorce". Both good songs. Both songs I liked. I thought to myself oh Elbow. I like them.

But I won't put them on the list.

I have heard the songs too many times.

The songs will follow me around.

Their lead singer has a radio show. He plays music I don't like.

They are also a bit calculated, formulaic, and predictable.

But I think, most importantly, it is because everyone else likes them and that is enough.

This list is about personal discovery, not public consensus. If a track is already soundtracking every stadium montage and weekend supermarket trip in the country, it certainly doesn't need my help. I want music that still feels like it belongs to me, rather than just serving as the polite background noise to everyone else's lives.

I think the moral of this story is that supermarkets need to play more Radiohead.

Ten Songs: Why things actually matter

July 13, 2026 Methodology By Josh

Ten songs is a harsh constraint, but a necessary one. Depending on the year you're looking at, hitting that exact number was either easy or a brutal exercise in "kill your darlings." Some years I genuinely struggled to find ten tracks, while others there were far too many!

The mid-to-late 1960s were particularly difficult for me to narrow down. This was the music I listened to growing up. Furthermore, it's the specific era that a lot of the bands I subsequently listened to during the 1990s were referencing, sampling, and pulling inspiration from. Whittling a momentous year like 1966 down was just rude. anyway!

But yeah, without the limit, an archive just becomes a playlist. It becomes passive. Forcing a ten-song cap means you have to make hard decisions about the narrative of that specific year. I have also tried to mold the 1000 songs as a narrative as a whole, so adding blue boy in 1997 looks back to Marlena Shaw's "Woman of the Ghetto" and that's on purpose!

Anchor Years: Why 1996 is the Center of the Archive

July 17, 2026 Methodology By Josh
Featured Artists: White Town Lew Stone Bronski Beat

When building a 100-year archive, you need a place to stand. A vantage point. It came about by trying to truly reconnect with music, searching for historical connection points. This is why I often suggest people start their journey through the archive in 1996.

1996 was a pivotal year for me personally, but more importantly, it serves as the perfect starting point for the entire project. Standing in the mid-90s allows you to look deep into the past, while simultaneously casting an eye forward into the digital future. Well, a little forward, anyway. Then we stop. We stop at 2030 because this archive is not an open-ended projection of the future; it is about encapsulating a highly specific, relatively short era: 1930 to 2030.

The anchor year is 1996. Consider how Your Woman by White Town sounds so intrinsically of its era—a bedroom-produced electronic hit—yet it relies entirely on a muffled, crackling trumpet sample.

That sample is ripped straight from the 1932 track My Woman by Lew Stone (featuring Al Bowlly). By placing these two tracks in the same overarching archive, suddenly 1996 and 1932 are inextricably linked. The archive isn't a straight, boring chronological line; it's a web of influences, much like how Bronski Beat laid the groundwork for the synth-pop that would follow them.

This is why understanding the lineage matters. A hundred years is enough time to watch these cultural echoes bounce back and forth.
