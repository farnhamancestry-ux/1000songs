Les Paul

24 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Les Paul

I read today that Les Paul's house was destroyed. His son, Gene, released a statement that really put into perspective what that space represented:

"That house wasn't just where Les Paul lived. It was his workshop. It was his laboratory. It was his journey of dreams. So much happened there.

This was where my father experimented endlessly with his guitars and electronics. It was where the first 8-track multitrack recorder that fundamentally changed modern recording was put to work, along with the first console used with it. It was a place where ideas were tried, rejected, rebuilt and tried again."

That statement shows just how important all these elements were for his genius to come together. It highlights how incremental technology developments, combined with sheer hard work and persistence, paved the way for the bands of the 1960s to develop rock music.

Most people know Les Paul from the Gibson guitar. But that instrument essentially came about through a frustration in sound, his search to eliminate feedback and create solid sustain. It ultimately produced those iconic images of bands playing their "cherry red" Gibsons. You really can't imagine the classic rock pose without it.

When I was going through the 1940s for this blog, I found a track by him from 1948 called "Lover."

Lover by Les Paul

For me, you can hear him desperately trying to innovate with sound, but you can also hear the technology holding him back. Because tape recorders weren't standard yet, he had to overlay eight separate electric guitar parts onto fragile acetate discs. He even recorded parts at half-speed so they would play back at a dizzying, fast pace. He went through nearly 500 ruined discs before he got it right—prompting comedian W.C. Fields to famously tell him, "My boy, you sound like an octopus."

In terms of innovation, his contribution desperately needs to be celebrated. I think for 1948 as well, "Lover" is a brilliant track. Les Paul remains the only person inducted into both the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame and the National Inventors Hall of Fame, proving that his relentless studio tinkering changed the trajectory of modern music forever.

Washboard Sam

23 August 2026 Artist By Josh

I knew I liked 1960s music. I knew 60s bands would often wear their 20s and 30s blues influences on their sleeves. It's kind of like how bands today declare their love for Oasis.

So, my 1930s choices have often been guided by that. Find 1960s songs I liked, then find their ancestors.

Washboard Sam is truly the great grandfather of a lot of music I love.

Washboard Sam was born Robert Clifford Brown. His exact origins are a bit of a blues mystery, many sources cite his birth in Walnut Ridge, Arkansas in 1910, though some researchers point to Jackson, Tennessee as early as 1903.

Before recording himself, he cut his teeth in the 1920s as a Memphis street musician. He eventually moved to Chicago in 1932, where he became a fixture of the scene playing alongside his reputed half-brother, blues legend Big Bill Broonzy (who also features in 1000 Songs).

The tunes I have gone for are "Don't Tear My Clothes," released in 1936, and "Let Me Play Your Vendor." from '42.

"Don't Tear My Clothes" is a great piece of acoustic Chicago blues. It's driven by Washboard Sam's strong voice and it sounds big despite the recording limitations of the era.

Don't Tear My Clothes by Washboard Sam

Its traditional melody and chord progression were later adapted by The Animals for their 1964 hit, "Baby Let Me Take You Home."

Baby Let You Take Me Home by The Animals

"Let Me Play Your Vendor" also has that brilliant Washboard Sam sound. It was another one that the British Invasion bands of the 60s.

Let Me Play Your Vendor by Washboard Sam

Washboard Sam was a revered musician. He died in 1966 and was buried in an unmarked grave. However, in 2009 Steve Salter organised a concert in order to raise money for a headstone to be erected. Thankfully so!

Dodgy

23 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Dodgy

My early music buying experience was shaped by a slightly toxic relationship with the Britannia Music Club.

They operated on the classic gateway model. Get a few free CDs or tapes then spend the next few years trying to figure out how to cancel while unwanted albums continuously arrived.

"Free Peace Sweet" by Dodgy was one such album I obtained via this method (coincidentally, Dodgy was also the legal status of every Britannia Music Club subscription contract ever signed.)

Dodgy were from Hounslow (someone has to be). They were bright sunny unashamed Britpop. They released singles like "Good Enough" and "Staying Out for the Summer."

"Free Peace Sweet" was an album that was ridiculously easy to listen to. A bit too easy, maybe.

But I have taken the plunge and gone with "In a Room."

In a Room by Dodgy

It described an era and I have declined from featuring other bands from that time like Cast, Bluetones, Space and the mighty Shed Seven!

The Auteurs

23 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: The Auteurs

If music didn't exist. If there were no songs. If there was only one substantial form of song that could be produced like a car or aeroplane I'd be quite happy if it was Lenny Valentino by The Auteurs.

Lenny Valentino by The Auteurs

The Auteurs were essentially a vehicle for Luke Haines. Haines was an uncompromising, sharp-tongued songwriter who formed the group in 1991 after leaving The Servants. Even after their 1993 debut "New Wave" picked up a Mercury Prize nomination, Haines preferred throwing barbs at the rising Britpop elite over joining their parade, keeping the band firmly on the margins.

Technically, the single was released '93, but moving it into '94 meant I could fit two PJ Harvey tracks into 1993!

1994 fits because it is the opening track to their second album, "Now I'm a Cowboy".

I think thing I like most about Lenny Valentino is the changes and the variation. It is a wave song.

Build. Through dead pan vocals.

Crash. Guitar and James Banbury's cello.

Interestingly, in Luke Haines' biography he never mentions James Banbury by name. He just calls him the cellist. Guess they didn't particularly get on.

But tension and release is why it's in 1000 songs. Absolute template for it.

Squeeze

22 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Squeeze

So this band just Squeezed on to my list. Squeeze.

I don't like most of their music. I think sometimes you have to "be there" with certain bands or artists, and I wasn't. It always feels like they are trying too hard. I think they often feel like they are perhaps pretending to be something they are not.

But I don't doubt their influence. They were once hailed as the "heirs to Lennon and McCartney's throne" and basically laid the groundwork for Britpop. Guitar legends like Blur's Graham Coxon idolised them, and massive stadium acts like U2, R.E.M., and Dire Straits actually got their start opening for Squeeze back in the day.

Also Jools Holland was part of the original line-up.

But "Take Me I'm Yours" is one of my favourite songs.

Take Me I'm Yours by Squeeze

They made it around the time they were working with John Cale. BUT although Cale produced most of their debut album, the record label thought his recording of "Take Me I'm Yours" was completely uncommercial. So, the band scrapped it and ended up producing the hit version of the track entirely by themselves. Which you have got to give them huge credit for.

What I love about the song is how entirely different it feels from their usual pub-rock pop. It has a synth hook. It's gritty, cynical and effortless in a way that their other tracks sometimes miss.

John Leyton

22 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: John Leyton

A song that sounds a bit like a horse. Check.

A guy that sounds a bit like a cowboy. Check.

A high voice. Check.

I thought I'd struggle with pre 1965 songs but when you have heaters such as Johnny Remember Me then I should have had no fear.

Johnny Remember Me by John Leyton

Interestingly, and I only found this out recently, John Leyton was actually an actor. He was starring in the ITV drama series Harpers West One, where he played a pop singer named Johnny Saint-Cyr. He sung it on an episode, this caused sales of the song and it went to number 1.

The song actually came about because songwriter Geoff Goddard woke up inspired in the middle of the night, rushed to a tape recorder he kept by his bed, and hummed the whole thing into existence. It then produced by Joe Meek, who did what Joe Meek did best and turned the drum track into that galloping rhythm.*

The high pitched backing vocals are then sung by session singer Lissa Gray.

At the time, on the TV show Juke Box Jury, (the brilliant comic) Spike Milligan criticised it as the son of Ghost Riders of the Storm. As someone who has included versions of that song 3 times in a 1000 song list, I celebrate this!

Unbelievably, as I write this in 2026, John is now 90 and still going strong.

But it's in. It was covered by Jimmy Somerville, it's a bit weird, it's atmospheric, it's dark and it was pop.

* (John Leyton actually appeared as a cameo in the 2009 film about Joe Meek. I haven't included Joe Meeks most famous song Telstar. I find it too annoying unfortunately.)

The Communards

21 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: The Communards

Noodle Doodles were a canned pasta snack made by Heinz. In 1987 they had promotion which you could send off for a free music cassette tape.

So, if this journal was about what I was listening to in the years selected I'd have to include Party Time with Noodle Doodles for the late 80s and early 90s.

That tape contained some very bad music, by Black Lace. However, I did have an antidote. "Don't Leave Me This Way" by The Communards. Which I listened to in equal amounts.

Don't Leave Me This Way by The Communards

The magic of "Don't Leave Me This Way", a cover, relies heavily on guest vocalist Sarah Jane Morris. The track perfectly contrasted Morris's deep, rich contralto voice with Somerville's soaring, trademark falsetto.

And the fact it goes ahhhhh baby. Not just ah baby. Ahhhhhhhn baby. Also a key changed ahhhhhh baby.

During a Top of the Pops performance, the band decided to have a bit of fun with the mandatory miming. Somerville and Morris swapped roles on stage, so Jimmy mimed the booming deep notes while Sarah Jane lip-synced to Jimmy's bit.

But Jimmy is a truly great vocalist. As probably the more high profile partner in the Communards Richard Coles attests to.

I also included "Never Can Say Goodbye" on the list, which is a cover of the Jackson 5 classic.

Never Can Say Goodbye by The Communards

That 1987 album also features "For a Friend". Written in memory of their close friend Mark Ashton, a prominent gay rights activist whose story was later immortalized in the film Pride. The soundtrack of that film has overlaps with 1000 Songs.

The Communards were a brilliant alt pop band and probably don't get the recognition they deserve. Even if some of us did discover them while consuming tins of pasta full of dangerously high preservative!

DEADLETTER

20 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: DEADLETTER

Sax as a synth is 2026 for me.

You can hear this perfectly on "It Comes Creeping," a phenomenal, broody, chugging post-punk anthem by DEADLETTER.

It Comes Creeping by DEADLETTER

The band weaponizes the saxophone. It doesn't swing or soar.

It cuts.

Like a rhythmic saw, locking into the bassline and propelling the track forward.

Although we are only two-thirds of the way through the year, this is already a surefire pick for me for the ten songs for 2026.

The band initially started in Yorkshire before relocating to London. They released their album Existence Is Bliss in early 2026, which is a brilliant follow-up to their critically acclaimed 2024 debut, Hysterical Strength.

Lead vocalist Zac Lawrence can do no wrong in my eyes. I saw them at a BBC Introducing show a few years back, and he is a classic frontman in every sense. I would honestly watch him perform "Mary Had a Little Lamb," and it would still be a brilliant show. There is genuine belief and intensity in every single word and action during his performance.

This authenticity is exactly why I'm including "Fit for Work" in the archive. Despite being "small-p" political, you can tell they spotted an issue they cared about and wrote a song. You just know it comes from a totally authentic place.

Fit For Work by DEADLETTER

I have also gone for "Pop Culture Connoisseur," which I just find funny. I am not from Wisbech, Cambridgeshire, but I feel like I am being slightly poked fun at with this song, which I love. Further, I will never forget them playing at The Hundred cricket tournament. The broadcast camera cut to unexpected fan Phil Tufnell, who then waxed lyrical on live television about how much he enjoyed the band.

Pop Culture Connoisseur by DEADLETTER

So yes, I think they are a great band, and I am very much looking forward to seeing what else is coming from them.

Talk Talk

19 August 2026 Artist By Josh

Long before the internet, let alone annoying broadband companies, there was Talk Talk.

They initially started as a synth-pop act; in fact, their first self-titled song was actually a reworking of an earlier track by frontman Mark Hollis's former punk-era band, The Reaction.

Talk Talk by Talk Talk

This features the brilliant lyrics:

"All you do is talk to me / Talk, talk, talk, talk."

Thankfully, Mark Hollis despised the commercialization of his music, and actively fought his record label to prevent them from using Talk Talk's catalog in commercials. Phew.

I like those lyrics, though. Repetition is comforting, if nothing else.

Mark Hollis later evolved into the more profound with tracks like Life's What You Make It.

Life's What You Make It by Talk Talk

Which again uses repetition.

But repetition is comforting. (Did I say that before?)

After commercial success they meticulously recorded hours of experimental, improvised jazz to invent(?) post-rock with the album Spirit of Eden.

I think everyone in life should follow this pattern in whatever field they are in. Do something commercially successful, and then turn it completely on its head. After all, as a wise man once wrote:

"dum dum dum dum dum dum... life's what you make it."

Martyrs

18 August 2026 Discovery By Josh

The Cure and Stock Aitken Waterman are not names you often hear grouped together. In a list of 1,000 songs, you’d rarely find both side by side (er Josh check 1000songs.co.uk)

I was intrigued to hear how that crossover would look in a sonic Venn diagram. So it was fascinating to hear Martyrs' lead track, 'Drowning Days', off their new release, The Last Summer EP.

Martyrs are Jon Howells and Michael Hall, from Merthyr Tydfil, South Wales. Writing and producing out of an attic studio inside a faded Victorian hotel overlooking the coast, they make whatever they want, whenever they want. Like me, they are completely unbothered by genre constraints or modern streaming algorithms. It's quite easy to avoid them, just don't stream music!

And I can hear it!

On 'Drowning Days', it sounds like something Mark Hollis (of Talk Talk) might have made after spending a couple of weeks in the Balearics. It’s as if Gruff Rhys from Super Furry Animals teamed up with a Detroit techno pioneer like Derrick May to lay down an '80s New Wave dance floor anthem.

The song is about revisiting the cities, streets, clubs, and hotel foyers where their most magical memories were formed, only to realise those places were only ever special because of the people they shared them with.

All in all, it's a great listen, especially if you want to put the last 40 years of music into a cement mixer and use that mix to construct a sad but catchy summer banger!

The Last Summer EP by Martyrs was out August 12, 2026, on all streaming platforms (okay, stream this!).

Zora Kirin

17 August 2026 Discovery By Josh

I initially started 1000 Songs as a way of marking time in songs rather than years. The plan was to divide every year of my life into ten tracks, meaning by the time I hit 40, I was exactly 400 songs old. As I have said before on here, the conventional measurement of time is vastly overrated. It’s dry, boring, and misses the joy, fun, and hope in life!

Today’s artist is inspired by the exact same milestone in a beautifully positive way.

On Saturday Berlin-based Zora Kirin released her debut solo EP, The Bones Are Beautiful, exactly on her 40th birthday.

To mark the occasion, she hosted a "four for forty" release and birthday party at Speakeazy in Kreuzberg, alongside Gözde Berberoğlu and Hibana.

If you were dealing in Berlin clichés, you’d probably assume a 40th birthday release would be a punishing four to the floor forty techno marathon, but Zora has gone back to her folk roots for the ocassion.

I don't feature a lot of folk in the archive, but there is something very special about writing a genuinely good, profound and unpretentious folk record. Zora achieves exactly this, defining the EP as "hopecore". A realistic hope rooted in the belief that while life is fleeting, there is always beauty to be found.

Lyrically and conceptually, it is a very strong EP that charts the four seasons of a lifetime, with stripped back production that lets the vocal and guitar do the talking.

The EP begins in the spring of life with the bursting "Fly On," driven by a bold, confident guitar and vocal reminiscent of Odetta or a stripped-back PJ Harvey. Summer dawns with "Let's Play Pretend," a song with a great guitar motif and celebrating the joy of discovery. Then it's on to the autumn inspired "Step Forth," passing wisdom to your younger self. The vocal delivery on this one is a bit more vulnerable reminiscent of Dolores O'Riordan. Finally, winter falls with the subtle "Mulberry Tree," a love song from the grave.

Zora encourages us to live broadly, in all directions, for as long as you can. And I certainly agree!

The Bones Are Beautiful is a perfect sonic testament to that ethos. It's well worth taking the time to listen, reflect, and celebrate the seasons of your own life!

The Bones Are Beautiful by Zora Kirin was out on 15th August 2026.

Odetta

17 August 2026 Artist By Josh

Bob Dylan famously said he traded his electric guitar for an acoustic the moment he heard Odetta. (He might have had June Ritchie and the others he was ripping off in mind too!)

Janis Joplin imitated her. Considered the voice of the civil rights movement, Martin Luther King Jr. crowned her "The Queen of American Folk Music".

Born in Birmingham, Alabama, she moved to Los Angeles as a child and actually began formal operatic training at the age of thirteen.

So she had this, classically trained vocal power. She channeled that vocal control directly into her work folk, spirituals, and blues.

Was really great going through her back catalogue. For the archive, I went with "Wade in the Water" and "Gallows Pole".

The first is about liberation and is widely believed to have served as a coded message on the Underground Railroad, instructing escaping slaves to step into the water to throw tracking bloodhounds off their scent.

Wade in the Water by Odetta

Then there is "Gallows Pole,". I also have Lead Belly's version in the archive. It is a tense, driving folk-blues narrative about a condemned woman frantically pleading with her family to bring enough silver or gold to bribe the hangman. It is gritty, urgent, and exactly what I want!

Gallows Pole by Odetta

Cillian Murphy did a brilliant series for BBC 6 music called Limited Edition. He went for Every Night when featuring Odetta which shows another side. She had such amazing versatility there is definitely not one song that can capture her.

Every Night by Odetta

But for me Odetta is the sound of raw, genuine, undiluted authenticity. Just brilliant.

Graham Coxon

13 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Graham Coxon

For a fairly brief, surreal period of my life, I felt like I was living in an episode of Stella Street. For those who don't know, Stella Street was a brilliant BBC sitcom where musicians and celebrities lived on the same suburban street, going about their mundane, everyday business.

I'd walk into Sainsbury's in the morning and see Amy Winehouse still on a night out, or I'd be standing directly behind Bill Nighy while I was buying my broccoli. Or sitting in an, otherwise completely empty internet cafe that was occupied by no one except Rhys Ifans. Or going to the local pub and seeing Pete Doherty, Kelly Osbourne, Noel Fielding and Amy Winehouse (again).

But out of all these surreal encounters, the one that stands out the most was walking into the corner shop and bumping into Graham Coxon. Whenever I saw Graham, he always looked absolutely terrified to me.

He had the distinct look of a man who constantly feared I was going to corner him by the biscuits and start singing Parklife right into his face.

There was no need to be fearful, though.

If I had a bit more confidence back then, I wouldn't have sung Parklife, I would have sung Freakin' Out into his face instead.

Freakin Out by Graham Coxon

Graham is responsible for some of my favourite guitar riffs of all time, Popscene, Song 2, or Freakin Out.

It was a strange privilege occupying the same geographic area as him for a while, and I was even lucky enough to see him perform just down the road.

He wrote these riffs like he didn't really want to, or wasn't that bothered that he could do it. It is incredibly annoying when someone is so effortlessly good that they barely seem to break a sweat while churning out pure genius.

His solo career is nothing short of prolific, releasing a mountain of music since 1998, playing almost all the instruments himself, designing his own album art, and scoring soundtracks. He has never really stopped, right up to his 2026 album release, Castle Park. But I will be honest, a lot of his solo stuff mainly didn't sit right with me. I am a sucker for something catchy, and his solo discography often leans a bit too heavily into the experimental for my personal taste.

Freakin' Out is the exception to that rule. It bridges the gap between his noisy, indie-rock sensibilities and a proper riff.

Saying all this. As much as I respect their individual side projects and undeniable talents, I prefer Blur to just be Blur. I don't want cartoon based music from Damon. I don't want artisanal cheese or champagne lifestyles from Alex. And as much as I respect Graham's solo artistry, I prefer his guitar work. They are at their absolute best when the four of them are in a room together, making proper, brilliant guitar music.

And if I ever see him in the corner shop again, I will definitely continue to just let him buy his milk in peace.

Dead or Alive

13 August 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Dead or Alive

Here is my first out-and-out pop band for the 1000 Songs archive.

Growing up, Stock Aitken Waterman (SAW) dominated chart music.

I think it made me literally hate music as a child. 80s pop was oppressive weird and scary for me.

In hindsight, though, I think that childhood hatred ended up being a good thing. You need to know what’s bad to find out what’s good. I am thankful now that SAW were such great and prolific purveyors of garbage (and yes, I’ve deliberately used the Americanism there).

The catalyst that turned their fabricated sewer system on, however, was actually just a good song.

It came from Dead or Alive. "You Spin Me Round (Like a Record)".

You Spin Me Round by Dead or Alive

You know when you spin round like a record? Yeah that. Spinning round like a record. Right round. Not half way round. Right round.

Anyway...

It was SAW’s first UK Number 1, arguably kicking off their entire chart deluge.

Frontman Pete Burns was unique. Back when he worked at Probe Records in Liverpool, he used to terrify customers from behind the counter, famously refusing to sell records to shoppers if he didn't like their clothes or deemed them unworthy of the music.

Burns was never going to let the machine control him.

His authenticity directly countered their clean production. The result is high-energy synth-pop perfection. From the dramatic, operatic synth intro to the galloping hi-hats and Pete’s booming, theatrical vocal delivery, it bridges the fierce, menacing edge of post-punk with unadulterated joy.

More importantly, it has an edge that SAW simply couldn't polish away.

So, it’s included in 1000 Songs because it proves that even within the ultra-manufactured pop machine of the 1980s, genuine eccentricity and an undeniable hook is all you need.

Opus Kink

11 August 2026 Discovery By Josh

Forget politely knocking on the door of the 2026 selections for 1000 Songs. Opus Kink has just blown it off its hinges.

Not with dynamite, but with horns... dynamic, door-disrespecting horns.

I have absolutely loved hearing the single "Come Over, Do Me Wrong" pop up on the radio this year. Driven relentlessly by its brass section, the track doesn't let you off the hook, while Angus Rogers' vocals keep you locked in. It doesn't pause. It gnaws away at you, like they are determined to demonstrate this is a band doing something genuinely different.

Frontman Angus Rogers noted that the song explores the hallucinogenic qualities and toxic allure of a doomed love affair, a wild dive into the mad philosophical leap of not caring what someone does, as long as they do it to you.

For me, it feels as though the spirit of Jimmie Lunceford has rocked up in Brighton, nursing a hangover but ready to take on the world with his Jazznocracy. (I have said it before and will say it again: the best kind of 'nocracy.)

The sextet originally formed back in 2017. Like the finest album growers of ancient Greece, they correctly decided to wait nearly ten years to release their debut full-length. I think it has something to do with fermentation. Distillation. Brassmentation.

Produced alongside Grammy Award winner Craig Silvey, their debut 10-track LP, The Sweet Goodbye, arrived on 31 July 2026 via SO Recordings, and it is well worth the wait.

This is easily one of my top picks for 2026. The Sweet Goodbye is out now and they are touring!

Come Over, Do Me Wrong by Opus Kink

Geoffrey Lewis

8 August 2026 Discovery By Josh

It's great when I hear a song, enjoy it for its atmosphere, and then read how the artist came to it from a different angle.

"Paranoid" by Geoffrey Lewis is just that. It grabbed me this week. Although I can certainly hear the DNA of Sunny Day Real Estate or American Football in the song, the feel of the track is what drew me in.

"Paranoid" tackles heavy subject matter, it is inspired by the anxiety of growing up in a world where public violence has become normalised.

That rawness, directness and authenticity of the song are reflected in how the track was made. Entirely written, performed, recorded, and produced in his Nashville home studio, the song is built around an analog set up, a Korg drum machine, tube amplifiers, and live instruments. I think for me, this is where the feel comes from. Because of this direct artist led production style, the track reminds me of the proto-electronic acts like Suicide or Silver Apples. Similarly, although sonically different, it has the same kind of clash between guitars and electronics of Liars.

Geoffrey's college friends provide the bass and backing vocals, and I really love how the song builds from the initial guitar riff, layered with those understated but highly effective vocal backings. The custom Korg beat drives the song, while the guitar motif responds with a rhythmic edge.

Lewis sings with a measured, understated, quality that makes the paranoia feel even more intense. He keeps the emotion just below the surface, and that restraint is exactly what makes it engaging.

This song sounds exactly like its title. The song does, indeed, sound paranoid, in a good way!

Liars

They Threw Us All in a Trench and Stuck a Monument on Top has still got to be one of the greatest album titles of all time.

My first pick for the archive is the equally brilliantly named "Mr You're On Fire Mr".

Mr You're On Fire Mr by Liars

Scissor by Liars

Curated by Portishead and put together by All Tomorrow's Parties, it was a legendary gig. I'll likely keep referring back to that festival on this blog, but Liars were absolutely a highlight of the weekend!

The Cuts

6 August 2026 Discovery By Josh

I have been very lucky to attend some memorable gigs over the years. One surreal, brilliant experience was watching The Strokes while stood right in front of a massive dinosaur skeleton at the Natural History Museum in London.

Now, I don't have The Strokes in the 1000 Songs archive, but they were undeniably pivotal. They heralded a massive explosion of brilliant guitar music throughout the early to mid 2000s, freeing bands just to be bands and make catchy, guitar driven tunes without overthinking it.

Lord Kitchener

31 July 2026 Artist By Josh
Featured Artists: Lord Kitchener

If we are talking about songs that capture a specific moment in time, which is basically the entire point of this 1000 Songs project, it is genuinely impossible to ignore Aldwyn Roberts, better known to the world as Lord Kitchener, or simply "Kitch."

He wasn't just a musician; he was the undisputed Grand Master of Calypso.

There are three key things you really need to understand about him to get why he's so vital to this archive.

Firstly, the man literally soundtracked history. In 1948, he bought a ticket on the HMT Empire Windrush. When the ship pulled into Tilbury Docks, bringing some of the first post-war West Indian immigrants to the UK, a reporter for Pathé News shoved a microphone in his face. Right there, on the spot, Kitchener sang a specially written song into the camera. No studio, no auto-tune, just a man and a heavy dose of optimism stepping into the freezing English breeze. That song was "London Is the Place for Me."

Secondly, he became the absolute voice of those first 5,000 West Indian migrants. Imagine leaving the Caribbean for a grey, post-war Britain. His music was a vital lifeline. It spoke of a home they desperately missed, but also documented the reality of the life they were trying to build.

Thirdly, he was a relentless innovator. He didn't just have a brief stint in the UK and fade away. He eventually moved back to Trinidad and completely dominated the calypso scene. We're talking winning the Road March competition 10 times. He ran his own calypso tent, nurtured a whole new generation of artists, and basically helped pioneer the transition into Soca music in the 70s.

And of course I put in...

London is the Place for Me by Lord Kitchener

Time is songs. Songs are time

22 July 2026 Deep Dive By Josh

The great Sydney Bertram Carter poetically wrote,

"Time is a thing like a bird on a wing, coming or going away."

I have gone with: time = songs.

Time has always been measured in different ways, whether it was the ancient Egyptians who measured time by tracking the stars and inventing the first sundials, or the French revolutionaries who completely changed the calendar and tried to force everyone to use ten-hour decimal clocks.

Speaking of clocks, we have Chris Martin's great-great-grandfather, William Willett, who decided to mess up our sleep patterns forever more with daylight saving time. (Which is ironic, considering how many hours of sleep we've all lost just trying to figure out how to change the time on the oven.) And yes, no Coldplay in my thousand, by the way, but I did like it when they used to completely rip Radiohead off—they were good then.

Bigger Stronger by Coldplay

(I actually bought this . Then sold it. Last time I checked it was worth a bit!)

But yes, I have gone with the idea that time is 10 songs a year. If you are 1, you are 10 songs; if you are 10, you are 100 songs. It's a nice way of recording the years. A bit like an audio journal. You could think of those songs as a way of conjuring up a special memory of a special time, or you might just want to spin around. I think spinning around can happen at all ages. You might need a padded room, but it's possible.

I don't like genrefying things for this reason, because like all art or communication, you have what is spoken and what is received. Art is created, and art is interpreted.

So the feeling you may associate with a song is potentially completely separate from the intention and inspiration of the original artist.

Anchor Years: Why 1996 is the Center of the Archive

July 17, 2026 Methodology By Josh
Featured Artists: White Town Lew Stone Bronski Beat

When building a 100-year archive, you need a place to stand. A vantage point. It came about by trying to truly reconnect with music, searching for historical connection points. This is why I often suggest people start their journey through the archive in 1996.

1996 was a pivotal year for me personally, but more importantly, it serves as the perfect starting point for the entire project. Standing in the mid-90s allows you to look deep into the past, while simultaneously casting an eye forward into the digital future. Well, a little forward, anyway. Then we stop. We stop at 2030 because this archive is not an open-ended projection of the future; it is about encapsulating a highly specific, relatively short era: 1930 to 2030.

The anchor year is 1996. Consider how Your Woman by White Town sounds so intrinsically of its era—a bedroom-produced electronic hit—yet it relies entirely on a muffled, crackling trumpet sample.

That sample is ripped straight from the 1932 track My Woman by Lew Stone (featuring Al Bowlly). By placing these two tracks in the same overarching archive, suddenly 1996 and 1932 are inextricably linked. The archive isn't a straight, boring chronological line; it's a web of influences, much like how Bronski Beat laid the groundwork for the synth-pop that would follow them.

This is why understanding the lineage matters. A hundred years is enough time to watch these cultural echoes bounce back and forth.
